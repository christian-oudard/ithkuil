# Grammatical Explanations

Quijada's grammar says what each category *is*. This file says how each
one lands in English, which is a different question and one the source
does not set out to answer. Every entry here is authored, and none of it
is a transcription: `reference/morphology.md` is the transcription, and stays
faithful to the PDF even where the PDF is wrong. Nothing here overrides
it, and where the two disagree the transcription is what the language
says.

The entries were written for machine translation, by running a gloss
through a model, comparing the output against Quijada's own English, and
recording what the model got wrong about the category that failed. That
provenance is why so many of them are negative: "do not add a separate
'I perceive that...' clause" is the residue of a model that kept adding
one. Read them as corrections to a plausible misreading, not as the
category's definition.

The entries come from the IthkuilTranslator project, merged here with
its author's permission. The descriptions are Quijada's, under the reuse
terms published on ithkuil.net; the guidance is that project's own.

Only categories that earned an entry appear, 202 of them. A category
with nothing surprising about it is absent, and that is not an oversight.

## Configuration

**DPX** duplex (§3.1)

binary or two-halved in nature — a configurational set of exactly two
entities, events, or states viewed as a paired or bipartite whole

*Translation:* duplex pair: prefer 'a pair of X' / 'two X as a unit'
(e.g. 'a pair of boots')—not bare plural

**DSS** duplex/similar/separate (§3.1)

Two entities, similar and physically separate

*Translation:* duplex similar separate: two alike items as a pair but
not joined (e.g. 'a pair of matching cups kept apart')

**DSC** duplex/similar/connected (§3.1)

Two entities, similar and connected

*Translation:* duplex similar connected: two alike items linked as a
pair (e.g. 'a pair of linked rings')

**DSF** duplex/similar/fused (§3.1)

Two entities, similar and fused

*Translation:* duplex similar fused: two alike items fused into one
pair-unit (e.g. 'a fused pair of identical plates')

**DDS** duplex/dissimilar/separate (§3.1)

Two entities, dissimilar and physically separate

*Translation:* duplex dissimilar separate: two unlike items as a loose
pair (e.g. 'a mismatched pair of gloves')

**MSS** multiplex/similar/separate (§3.1)

Three or more entities, similar and physically separate — a multiplex
set whose members are alike but not physically connected

*Translation:* multiplex similar separate: many alike items, unjoined
(e.g. 'a loose set of matching tiles')

**MSC** multiplex/similar/connected (§3.1)

Three or more entities, similar and connected — a multiplex set whose
members are alike and physically touching or linked

*Translation:* multiplex similar connected: many alike items
touching/linked (e.g. 'a chain of identical beads')

**MSF** multiplex/similar/fused (§3.1)

Three or more entities, similar and fused

*Translation:* multiplex similar fused: many alike items fused (e.g. 'a
fused cluster of identical crystals')

**MDS** multiplex/dissimilar/separate (§3.1)

Three or more entities, dissimilar and physically separate — a multiplex
set whose members differ from one another and are not physically
connected

*Translation:* multiplex dissimilar separate: many unlike items,
unjoined (e.g. 'a mixed pile of tools')

**MDC** multiplex/dissimilar/connected (§3.1)

Three or more entities, dissimilar and connected — a multiplex set whose
members differ from one another but are physically touching or linked

*Translation:* multiplex dissimilar connected: many unlike items
linked/touching (e.g. 'linked odd-sized modules')

**MDF** multiplex/dissimilar/fused (§3.1)

Three or more entities, dissimilar and fused

*Translation:* multiplex dissimilar fused: many unlike items fused (e.g.
'a fused mass of mixed scrap')

**MFS** multiplex/fuzzy/separate (§3.1)

Three or more entities, fuzzy in similarity and physically separate

*Translation:* multiplex fuzzy separate: loosely similar-looking items,
unjoined (e.g. 'assorted vaguely round pebbles, some more than others')

**MFC** multiplex/fuzzy/connected (§3.1)

Three or more entities, fuzzy in similarity and connected

*Translation:* multiplex fuzzy connected: loosely similar-looking items
linked (e.g. 'linked vaguely rounded beads, some more than others')

## Perspective

**N** nomic (§3.3.3)

generic collective entity or archetype, containing all members or
instantiations of a configurative set throughout space and time

*Translation:* nomic generic: with 'child'-N or creature/person stems
prefer plural generic subjects (e.g. 'children'); with habit/verb stems
prefer generic/habitual practice—not a one-off deictic instance and do
not invent a bare 'they' subject from N alone

## Extension

**PRX** proximal (§3.4.2)

indicates that a noun is being spoken of not in its entirety, but rather
only in terms of the portion, duration, subset, or aspect which is
relevant to the context at hand

*Translation:* when PRX marks the main eventive formative with no
case-marked actor, prefer impersonal 'takes place' / 'going on' over
calquing 'stretch/portion of'; when PRX marks a noun, keep natural
existential English (e.g. 'if there's an X')

## Essence

**NRM** normal (§3.5)

Real-world actualities — the formative denotes entities, acts, or states
taken as actually existing in the real world rather than imagined or
representative counterparts

*Translation:* normal/real-world framing: prefer a plain factual clause
(e.g. 'the guard notices a shadow')—not hypothetical/representative
wording

**RPV** representative (§3.5)

Alternative/imagined counterparts — the formative denotes entities,
acts, or states as imagined, hypothetical, or representative rather than
as real-world actualities

*Translation:* hypothetical/representative—not always agent "imagine";
with Nomic participants (generic person/creature stems), prefer
infinitival/generic framing (e.g. "for an X to VERB a Y")—do not add
"imagine having…"

## Context

**EXS** existential (§3.9.1)

ontologically objective features that exist irrespective of observers,
opinions, or interpretations

*Translation:* existential context: render as a bare physical scene
(e.g. 'rain falls on the roof') with no social/metaphorical overlay

**FNC** functional (§3.9.2)

features defined socially by ideas, attitudes, beliefs, opinions,
convention, cultural status, use, function, benefit

*Translation:* functional context: highlight personal/social
significance of the scene (e.g. 'the letter arriving mattered for the
family')

**RPS** representational (§3.9.3)

symbol, metaphor, or metonym, representing some other concept or entity
abstractly associated with it

*Translation:* representational context: treat participants as
metaphorical/stand-ins (e.g. 'the storm stood for the argument')

**AMG** amalgamative (§3.9.4)

systemic, holistic, gestalt-like nature derived from interrelationships
between all parts and developmental history

*Translation:* amalgamative context: blend emotional impact with
cultural meaning (e.g. 'the ceremony hit hard and carried tradition')

## Transrelative Cases

**STM** stimulative (§4.2.5)

The party/entity/idea/thought/situation or mental state which triggers
an unwilled, affective response or is the trigger for an existential
states with no tangibly affected patient, e.g., The hammerexemplifies
toolship, The man possesses a cat, That book contains poems. Its
semantic role is that of STIMULUS.

*Translation:* When the stimulus is an instance of musical performance
framing attendance at a scheduled event, English often synthesizes as
"concert".

**IND** inducive (§4.2.9)

The patient who undergoes the tangible effect, impact, or change of
state of an act/event initiated/caused by that self-same party. Its
semantic role is that of simultaneous AGENT and PATIENT.

*Translation:* IND marks the self-affected subject of the predicate (who
acts and undergoes)—not manner or simile; prefer a subject NP (e.g. 'the
boys run') over 'in the manner of X'

## Appositive Cases

**POS** possessive (§4.3.1)

A noun which has alienable (removable or severable) possession of
another noun in the sense of having physical control or oversight of
that noun, whether by chance circumstance or deliberate manipulation.

*Translation:* temporary/physical possession in use: prefer 'X's Y' for
the item currently used/occupied (e.g. 'the rider's saddle [in
use]')—not ownership-as-property

**PRP** proprietive (§4.3.2)

A noun having alienable possession of another noun in the sense of
quasi-permanent contextual control, ownership or oversight, whether by
societal recognition, social convention, law, purchase or decree.

*Translation:* proprietive ownership: prefer owned-property reading
(e.g. 'the farmer's tractor')—contrast POS (in-use)

**GEN** genitive (§4.3.3)

A noun which has inalienable (unremovable, unseverable) possession of or
association with another noun in the sense of having that noun as an
inherent or intrinsic attribute, characteristic, physical part, or
genetic (familial) bond.

*Translation:* intrinsic/whole-part genitive: prefer inalienable or
constitutive 'of' (e.g. 'the lid of a jar' / 'the river's bank')

**ATT** attributive (§4.3.4)

A noun which inalienably experiences the effects of, or otherwise has an
affective relationship with another noun, either as a temporary or
permanent attribute, characteristic, or experience, whether physical or
psychological, objective or subjective in nature.

*Translation:* attributive quality as possessed trait: prefer 'X's Y'
for a quality/emotion (e.g. 'the singer's nervousness')

**PDC** productive (§4.3.5)

The creator, author or originator of another noun.

*Translation:* productive origin: prefer creator→product (e.g. 'the
poet's essays')

**ITP** interpretative (§4.3.6)

A noun acting as the subjective interpretational context of another
noun, that is the noun by or through which another noun is subjectively
interpreted or described.

*Translation:* interpretative viewpoint: prefer 'from X's point of view'
/ 'in X's reading' (e.g. 'from the judge's standpoint')

**OGN** originative (§4.3.7)

A noun as being the literal or figurative source of another, or which is
the native location, origin, or usual locational context for another.

*Translation:* originative source: prefer 'fruit/product of X' origin
(e.g. 'honey from the hive')

**IDP** interdependent (§4.3.8)

A noun which has a coordinated, tandem, complementary or mutually
dependent relationship with another. The relationship between the two
nouns can be thought of as reciprocal in nature.

*Translation:* interdependent part-whole: prefer constitutive
containment (e.g. 'the marrow in the bone')

**PAR** partitive (§4.3.9)

A quantitative or content-to-container relationship between the two
nouns. Also used to mark a noun qualified by a specific number.

*Translation:* partitive measure/group-of: prefer 'a load/batch of X'
(e.g. 'a crate of bottles')

## Associative Cases

**APL** applicative (§4.4.1)

A noun which represents the purpose for which another noun is to be
utilized in a given instance. Usually translates English 'for' when
meaning 'for the temporary or incidental use/purpose of.'

*Translation:* With reciprocal social introduction, often synthesizes as
purpose "to meet X".

**PUR** purposive (§4.4.2)

A noun which characterizes or defines the purpose of, or reason for,
another noun. Defines another noun's dedicated function or primary
reason for being, outside of any contextual instance.

*Translation:* purposive function-as-name: English often names the tool
by purpose (e.g. 'a watering can')

**TRA** transmissive (§4.4.3)

A noun for whose sake or benefit an action or event occurs or is done or
the party to whose detriment an action or event occurs.

*Translation:* transmissive beneficiary/recipient of transfer: prefer 'X
for Y' as intended recipient (e.g. 'a letter for the neighbor')

**DFR** deferential (§4.4.4)

Translates the English phrases 'out of respect for,' 'for the sake of,'
or 'in deference toward,' identifying the noun to which deference is
paid within the context for an act, condition, or event.

*Translation:* deferential motive: prefer 'out of respect for X' / 'in
deference to X' (e.g. 'out of respect for custom')

**CRS** contrastive (§4.4.5)

A noun which has been replaced by another or for which another noun has
been substituted. Translatable by the English phrases 'instead of' or
'rather than' or 'as opposed to.'

*Translation:* contrastive not-that: prefer 'X, not Y' / 'rather than'
(e.g. 'it's tea, not coffee')

**TSP** transpositive (§4.4.6)

Implies substitution of one noun for another. Translates English 'for'
in the sense of 'on behalf of,' 'in place of,' or 'instead of' (i.e.,
'as a substitution for').

*Translation:* transpositive substitution: prefer 'substituting X for Y'
/ 'replacing Y with X' (e.g. 'substituting ink for paint')

**CMM** commutative (§4.4.7)

Translates English 'for' in the sense of 'in exchange for.'

*Translation:* commutative exchange/payment framing: prefer
'paid/exchanged for X' (e.g. 'they paid for the lessons')

**CMP** comparative (§4.4.8)

A noun being compared to another. Translates such expressions as 'as
compared to,' or certain usages of 'versus' or 'as opposed to.'

*Translation:* comparative standard: prefer 'X compared to Y' / 'X
relative to Y' (e.g. 'bitter compared to honey')

**CSD** considerative (§4.4.9)

Translates English terms such as 'according to,' 'in the opinion of.'

*Translation:* considerative authority: prefer 'according to X' / 'in
X's judgment' (e.g. 'according to the guide')

## Adverbial Cases

**FUN** functive (§4.5.1)

Functions similarly to English adverbs ending in -ly or the adverbial
use of with, identifying the manner in which an action, event, or state
occurs or exists.

*Translation:* functive manner: prefer manner adverb/PP of how the act
is done (e.g. 'speaks confidently')

**TFM** transformative (§4.5.2)

The outcome or final state of a process, often translatable by 'to,'
'until,' or 'into' in the sense of reaching a final state after having
undergone some transformation.

*Translation:* transformative end-state as purpose of the act: prefer
'lessons/training into becoming X' reading—not mere payment-for

**CLA** classificative (§4.5.3)

A noun as a basis for arranging, sorting, classifying, or counting. Also
identifies a noun considered in consecutively recurring increments as a
means of describing the manner of an event.

*Translation:* classificative by class/unit: prefer 'X by X' / 'X-wise'
/ 'by the X' (e.g. 'page by page' / 'widthwise')

**RSL** resultative (§4.5.4)

A result or consequence, usually translatable utilizing a clause-like
structure in English.

*Translation:* when chaining AVS/EML avoidance, prefer infinitive
purpose 'to avoid being turned into X'—not 'resulting in avoiding…'

**CSM** consumptive (§4.5.5)

The resource consumed or used (up) as a result of, or as a concurrent
part of a process.

*Translation:* consumptive means/ingredient: prefer 'with/using X as
consumed means' (e.g. 'bakes bread with yeast')

**CON** concessive (§4.5.6)

A noun, situation, or circumstance which gives rise to an expectation of
a certain result which, in fact, does not occur.

*Translation:* concessive despite: prefer 'in spite of X' / 'despite X'
(e.g. 'in spite of the delay')

**AVR** aversive (§4.5.7)

A noun as a source or object of fear and/or avoidance. Translates
expressions such as 'for fear of,' 'in order to avoid,' or 'in avoidance
of.'

*Translation:* aversive fear-motive: prefer 'out of fear of X' / 'for
fear of X' (e.g. 'out of fear of silence')

**CVS** conversive (§4.5.8)

Used in conjunction with hypothetical or potential contexts to identify
a hypothetical exception to a potential outcome or an actual
circumstance which alters or has altered a potential outcome.

*Translation:* conversive exception: prefer 'except X' / 'all but X'
(e.g. 'everything was packed except the lamp')

**SIT** situative (§4.5.9)

A noun as the background context for a clause without implying a direct
causative relationship between the background context and the act,
condition, or event which occurs.

*Translation:* situative because-of-presence: cause is X's
presence/existence, not an act by X (e.g. 'upset because of the crowd
[being there]')

## Relational Cases

**PRN** pertinential (§4.6.1)

A formative functioning as the general referent of the verb, translating
such English terms as 'about,' 'regarding,' 'concerning,' 'in regard
to,' 'in reference to,' 'pertaining to,' or 'as for.'

*Translation:* Marks the about-topic; other quoted stems in the same
formative may be distinct participants.

**DSP** descriptive (§4.6.2)

A formative as describing another formative in an adjectival manner,
translatable as 'characterised/describable as being like',
'characterized/describable as having the quality/qualities of'.

*Translation:* descriptive case-frame: relative/descriptive clause
characterizing the head (e.g. 'bird-watching hikers tend to wake
early')—do not promote the frame to a main-clause only reading

**COR** correlative (§4.6.3)

An abstract general relationship, association, or conjunction between
one formative and another, including subjective, contextual,
metaphorical, or symbolic associations.

*Translation:* correlative association/theme: prefer 'in the direction
of X' or 'X in Y' thematic link (e.g. 'symbols in folklore')

**CPS** compositive (§4.6.4)

A formative as being the literal or figurative substance or component(s)
of which another is made, composed, formed, built or comprised.

*Translation:* compositive material: prefer 'made of X' / 'of X' (e.g.
'things made of glass')

**COM** comitative (§4.6.5)

A formative that accompanies another, translatable as '(along) with'.

*Translation:* comitative accompaniment (not worn): prefer 'with X' as
accompanying object carried along (e.g. 'a traveler with a
suitcase')—contrast UTL

**UTL** utilitative (§4.6.6)

A formative in the process of being used while some other activity or
state is in progress.

*Translation:* utilitative worn/in-use tool: prefer 'wearing/using X'
(e.g. 'a traveler wearing a coat')—contrast COM

**PRD** predicative (§4.6.7)

The non-causal basis, foundation, sustenance (literally or
figuratively), or required existential condition for another noun or
clause, translatable by the phrases 'based (up)on,' 'dependent (up)on'
or 'relying on.'

*Translation:* predicative basis: prefer 'based in/on X' (e.g. 'rules
based in evidence')

## Affinitive Cases

**ACT** Activative (§4.7.1)

Identifies the experiencer of a modal state, such as obligation,
necessity, desire, hope, expectation, wishing, being able to, etc.
functioning as the "subject" of a modal(ized) verb governing a
hypothetical state (i.e., what is wanted, needed, expected, able to be
done, etc.).

*Translation:* activative enablement/activation: often a modal 'even X
needs Y' / enabling frame (e.g. 'I think even machines need care')—do
not drop the validating matrix if present

**ASI** Assimilative (§4.7.2)

Identifies a formative used as a context for analogy or metaphorical
comparison, translatable as 'as/like' meaning 'as if it were X'

*Translation:* assimilative likeness without identity: prefer 'like an X
(though not one)' (e.g. 'you argue like a lawyer [though you are not]')

**ESS** Essive (§4.7.3)

Identifies the role or name by which an entity is known or contextually
identified, translates 'as/like' in sense of naming or functional
identity

*Translation:* essive identity-as: prefer 'like an X (because you are
one)' / 'as an X' (e.g. 'you argue like a lawyer [being one]')

**TRM** Terminative (§4.7.4)

Identifies a formative as being the goal of an act/event

*Translation:* terminative goal of seeking: prefer bare goal NP of
pursuit (e.g. 'we're seeking mushrooms')

**SEL** Selective (§4.7.5)

Identifies a contextually recurring time-period or string of recurring
entities

*Translation:* selective every-nth / subset pick: prefer 'every nth X' /
'each selected X' (e.g. 'every fourth row')

**CFM** Conformative (§4.7.6)

Identifies the entity pursuant to which, as per which, or in conformance
with which another entity is or an act/event occurs

*Translation:* On a prior formative, English "Under / pursuant to X"
frames the following modal act; do not make X the direct object of the
verb in the next formative unless the gloss marks object case on X.

**DEP** Dependent (§4.7.7)

Identifies a formative as being the basis of a dependency phrase on
which another formative acts as contingency

*Translation:* dependent conditional circumstance: prefer 'only if X' /
'provided X' (e.g. 'will attend only if there is music')

## Spatio-Temporal Cases — Group I

**LOC** Locative (§4.8.1)

Identifies the location where something is situated or occurs,
translates 'at/in/on/by'

*Translation:* locative at/in place: prefer 'at/in X' static location
(e.g. 'at the gate')

**ALL** Allative (§4.8.3)

Identifies the entity toward which another entity is moving/approaching,
translates 'to/toward'

*Translation:* allative toward goal: prefer 'to(ward) X' (e.g. 'toward
the harbor')

**ABL** Ablative (§4.8.4)

Identifies the entity away from which another entity is moving/receding,
translates '(away) from'

*Translation:* ablative away-from source: prefer 'away from X' / 'from
X' (e.g. 'walk away from the platform')

**ORI** Orientative (§4.8.5)

Identifies the entity (often a body part or sub-component) which serves
as the "face" or surface or "front" or "head" of an entity in terms of
external communication or interaction, or which serves as the forward
"end" of a spatially orientated axis aligned to a vector of motion in
terms of directional movement

*Translation:* with motion verbs + body-part/orientation stems (front,
back, flank/side), prefer directional adverbs (frontward, backward,
sideways)—not path PPs (along the side / toward the back)

**IRL** Interrelative (§4.8.6)

Identifies the directional/temporal orientation or position of an entity
relative to another

*Translation:* interrelative temporal relation focus: keep the
between-times relation (e.g. 'how long since the train's arrival?' as
relation-between-times)—not mere clock time

**INV** Intrative (§4.8.7)

Identifies a formative as being the spatio-temporal boundary point of a
span or volume of space or period of time

*Translation:* intrative juxtaposition of two times/events: focus on the
span between endpoints (e.g. 'how long between departure and return')

**NAV** Navigative (§4.8.8)

Identifies the entity whose literal or metaphorically inferred long axis
serves as the direction of another entity's path or trajectory

*Translation:* navigative path/way followed: prefer 'follow the way/path
of X' (e.g. 'they follow the river's course')

## Spatio-Temporal Cases — Group II

**ASS** Assessive (§4.9.2)

Identifies the increment of space or time or unit by which a contextual
ratio of measurement is created

*Translation:* assessive rate/ratio: prefer 'X per Y' / 'X for each Y'

**PER** Periodic (§4.9.3)

Identifies the span of time/time frame at some point(s) during which
separate events, repetitions, or durationally segmented acts or states
take place

*Translation:* time frame for a (segmented) act's completion: prefer "it
takes X" / "in X" / "within X"—do not render as a past-event setting
adjunct "over an X period"; contrast PRO ("for an hour" = how long an
act lasts)

**PRO** Prolapsive (§4.9.4)

Identifies the duration of an act, condition, or event, i.e. how long it
takes or lasts

*Translation:* how long an act lasts: prefer "for X" / "through X" /
"all X"—contrast PER ("it takes X" / "in/within X" for
time-to-completion)

**PCR** Postcursive (§4.9.6)

Identifies an entity acting as a point in time after which an act,
state, or event occurs

*Translation:* point-in-time after which: keep "after …" / "it was after
…"; with RTR+CRL (outburst) formative, prefer PCR as a past-event frame
over a bare ongoing "there's a … going on"; when FRAMED attend/event
precedes, keep attendees + attend verb

## Valence

**MNO** Monoactive (§5.1.1)

The default valence indicating a lack of co-activity, no participation
by a second party is implied

*Translation:* monoactive: no co-activity implied; parties may share a
scene without interacting (e.g. 'two bakers work in the kitchen [each on
their own]')

**PRL** Parallel (§5.1.2)

Indicates that a second party is engaging in the same activity as the
first party at same time

*Translation:* parallel: same kind of activity at the same time,
separately (e.g. 'two bakers knead dough at the same time, separately')

**CRO** Corollary (§5.1.3)

Similar to parallel, except that the second party engages in related
activity at the same time as the first party

*Translation:* corollary: simultaneous related but different activities
(e.g. 'two bakers work at once, each on a different recipe')

**RCP** Reciprocal (§5.1.4)

Indicates identical activity by one party directed at another party and
vice-versa. English may be 'each other'/'one another' when the
reciprocal partners are a named closed set (often SBS-marked), or
reciprocity with an unnamed external party when only a single group
party is named without SBS.

*Translation:* If two or more parties are separately named (distinct
formatives/stems), render reciprocity among those named parties. If a
single plural/group/duplex party is named without SBS, do not say 'each
other'—imply an unnamed external reciprocal partner (e.g. 'playing with
another party', not 'with each other'). SBS1-1 on that group marks
closed-set 'each other' within the group. A separate PRN formative is
the about-topic. Pattern: DPX group-IND + RCP without SBS → reciprocity
with an unnamed external party; same gloss + SBS1-1 → closed-set 'with
each other'.

**CPL** Complementary (§5.1.5)

Indicates that the second party performs a complementary activity to
that of the first party, the "other half" of the joint activity

*Translation:* complementary: interlocking roles of one joint activity
(e.g. 'two bakers run a complementary bake—one mixes, one shapes')

**DUP** Duplicative (§5.1.6)

Indicates that the second party copies or repeats the activity of the
first party

*Translation:* duplicative: one party does the act and the other copies
it (e.g. 'one baker demonstrates kneading and the other copies')

**DEM** Demonstrative (§5.1.7)

Indicates that the first party demonstrates for the second party how to
do something or what to do

*Translation:* demonstrative: one shows the other how (e.g. 'one baker
shows the other how to score the loaf')

**CNG** Contingent (§5.1.8)

Indicates that the second party engages in the next or dependent phase
of a multi-part activity

*Translation:* Sequential/dependent phases of one multi-part activity:
one party completes one part, then the other does the next logical part.
Do not collapse to 'together'/'with each other' (RCP±SBS), simultaneous
complementary roles (CPL), or copying the same act (DUP). Prefer
explicit sequencing ('one … then the other … the next part'). E.g.
starting a campfire for a friend in CNG implies the friend then cooks.

**PTI** Participative (§5.1.9)

Indicates that the parties take part in an activity involving a greater
whole, translatable by 'take part in'

*Translation:* participative: both take part in the same shared activity
(e.g. 'two bakers take part in the same bake')

## Phase

**PCT** Punctual (§5.3.1)

Describes an act, condition, or event which is point-like, momentary or
instantaneous in nature, such as an explosion, a flash of lightning, a
single handclap, or a single cough. It can be visually represented along
a timeline by a single point.

*Translation:* punctual: single brief occurrence (e.g. 'the bell gave
one short ring')

**ITR** Iterative (§5.3.2)

Refers to a momentary event which repeats itself in a rapid, on/off,
staccato manner, considered as a single event

*Translation:* iterative: a series of short occurrences (e.g. 'the bell
gave a series of short rings')

**REP** Repetitive (§5.3.3)

Refers to a relatively brief event repeated in an on/off staccato manner

*Translation:* repetitive: a series of longer occurrences (e.g. 'the
bell gave a series of long rings')

**ITM** Intermittent (§5.3.4)

Similar to iterative but the duration of time between repetitions is
relatively long and contextually relevant

*Translation:* intermittent: a repeating short occurrence pattern (e.g.
'the bell kept giving a repeating short ring')

**RCT** Recurrent (§5.3.5)

Indicates a slow repetition of an event, where the duration between
occurrences is relatively long

*Translation:* slow recurrence; with bouncing/jumping verbs, English
often uses '(up and down)' rather than the literal phrase 'slowly
recurring'

**FRE** Frequentative (§5.3.6)

Indicates an iterative occurrence which in turn repeats at intervals,
the whole considered as a single event

*Translation:* frequentative: repeating series of singles (e.g. 'the
bell gave repeating series of single rings')

**FRG** Fragmentative (§5.3.7)

Indicates a random pattern of punctual occurrences, the whole considered
as a single event

*Translation:* fragmentative: several random series of repeating shorts
(e.g. 'several random bursts of repeating short rings')

**VAC** Vacillitative (§5.3.8)

Functions as a non-punctual counterpart to the fragmentative

*Translation:* vacillitative: several random series of repeating longs
(e.g. 'several random bursts of repeating long rings')

**FLC** Fluctuative (§5.3.9)

Indicates a random pattern of both punctual and longer occurrences

*Translation:* random punctual bursts / snippets; with
sing.a.song+RSM+PRL, prefer fragmented/burst song snippets—not bare
'intermittently'

## Effect

**1:BEN** Beneficial to Speaker (§5.4.1)

Indicates that the formative is of benefit to the first-person speaker
of the sentence

*Translation:* when bundled as PA+1M/BEN (or similar
multiparty+speaker), benefit scopes over the whole party ("to our
benefit" / we)—not speaker-alone "to my benefit"

**2:BEN** Beneficial to Addressee (§5.4.2)

Indicates that the formative is of benefit to the addressee of the
sentence

*Translation:* beneficial to addressee: benefit scopes to 'you'/'your
side' (e.g. weather/news framed as good for the hearer)

**3:BEN** Beneficial to Third Party (§5.4.3)

Indicates that the formative is of benefit to a third party other than
the speaker or addressee

*Translation:* beneficial to a third party: benefit scopes to that third
party (e.g. 'the patient's recovery has returned' as good for them)

**SLF:BEN** Beneficial to Self (§5.4.4)

Indicates that the formative is of benefit to the formative itself to
which the affix is attached

*Translation:* beneficial to self: self-benefactive announcement/state
(e.g. present a favorable outcome for the speaker without a separate 'I
declare' matrix)

**UNK** Unknown Benefit (§5.4.5)

Indicates that the formative is either of unknown benefit or detriment
or that any benefit/detriment is irrelevant

*Translation:* unknown benefit/detriment: keep evaluative neutrality
(e.g. 'there's an odd object in the field' with no good/bad spin)

**SLF:DET** Detrimental to Self (§5.4.6)

Indicates that the formative is detrimental to the formative itself to
which the affix is attached

*Translation:* detrimental to self: self-directed negative evaluation
(e.g. self-reproach force without inventing extra parties)

**3:DET** Detrimental to Third Party (§5.4.7)

Indicates that the formative is detrimental to a third party other than
the speaker or addressee

*Translation:* detrimental to a third party: harm/ill scopes to that
party (e.g. 'the neighbor's debt has returned')

**2:DET** Detrimental to Addressee (§5.4.8)

Indicates that the formative is detrimental to the addressee of the
sentence

*Translation:* detrimental to addressee: bad-for-you scoping (e.g.
reported news framed as disadvantageous to the hearer)

**1:DET** Detrimental to Speaker (§5.4.9)

Indicates that the formative is detrimental to the first-person speaker
of the sentence

*Translation:* detrimental to speaker: bad-for-me scoping on the focused
content (e.g. identifying a situation as undesirable for the speaker)

## Level

**EQU** equative (§5.5.3)

X M 's the same as Y ( or as before) / X is as M as Y ( or as before)

*Translation:* equative level: prefer 'as … as' equality between
compared parties

**SUR** surpassive (§5.5.3)

X M 's more than Y ( or than before) / X is more M than Y ( or than
before)

*Translation:* surpassive relative greater degree: prefer comparative

## Aspect

**PCS** precessive (§5.6.6)

Conveys that an action, state or event has immediately preceded

*Translation:* precessive recent past: prefer 'just (now) …' (e.g. 'I
just noticed a stranger at the gate')

**REG** regulative (§5.6.7)

Conveys the idea of participation or involvement in an action, state, or
event over an amount of time extending from the past into the future
relative to the contextual present

*Translation:* regulative habitual ongoing: prefer 'have been …-ing
(everywhere/habitually)' (e.g. 'couriers have been busy delivering
parcels everywhere')

**SMM** summative (§5.6.8)

Translates English '...(have) already...'

*Translation:* summative already-complete: prefer 'have already finished
X' (e.g. 'they have already finished packing')

**CSS** cessative (§5.6.11)

Conveys the idea of cessation of an event, state or action

*Translation:* cessative stop: prefer 'stopped/ceased X-ing' (e.g. 'they
stopped packing')

**PAU** pausal (§5.6.12)

Indicates a pause in an action, state or event, with an implied
intention to resume

*Translation:* pausal pause-in: prefer 'paused in X-ing' (e.g. 'they
paused in packing')

**RGR** regressive (§5.6.13)

Conveys the idea of a return to an original or previous action, state or
event after a long hiatus involving an intervening change of state or
situation

*Translation:* regressive return-to: prefer 'got back to X' after
interruption (e.g. 'after the break, she got back to studying')

**PCL** preclusive (§5.6.14)

Conveys the fact that an action, state, or event takes place from
inception to conclusion all in one contextual segment

*Translation:* preclusive all-at-once/whole: prefer 'X whole' /
undivided completion (e.g. 'swallowed the pill whole')

**ICS** incessative (§5.6.16)

Conveys that an action, state or event continues on without stopping

*Translation:* incessative keeps-on: prefer 'keeps X-ing on and on'
(e.g. 'keeps rambling on and on')

**EXP** experiential (§5.6.17)

Translates English 'ever' in the sense of 'within the realm of one's
experience'

*Translation:* experiential ever-in-life: prefer 'Have you ever …?'
(e.g. 'Have you ever seen a comet?')

**IRP** interruptive (§5.6.18)

Translates English 'to get interrupted while X-ing' or 'to X only so far
or so much before having to stop'

*Translation:* interruptive broken-by: prefer 'was interrupted by X'
(e.g. 'the meeting was interrupted by an alarm')

**PMP** preemptive (§5.6.19)

Emphasizes the singularity and initial occurrence an action, state or
event

*Translation:* preemptive first-time/at-last milestone: prefer
'finally/for the first time' milestone reading (e.g. 'the apprentice
finally soloed')—not mere delay

**CLM** climactic (§5.6.20)

Emphasizes the finality of an action, state or event

*Translation:* climactic last-straw: prefer 'for the last time'
culminating warning (e.g. 'you've lied for the last time')

**DLT** dilatory (§5.6.21)

Translates English '(be) long-delay(ed) in being/doing X'

*Translation:* long delay: with CPT-'seek'+AFM, keep both long-delay and
certainty—do not collapse to bare 'finally'

**TMP** temporary (§5.6.22)

Conveys that an action, state or event is being considered or is
applicable only to the present subjective context

*Translation:* temporary present-context only: prefer "for the time
being" / "for now"—do not drop the temporary sense

**XPD** expenditive (§5.6.23)

Conveys an all-consuming action, state, or event which interferes with
or prevents other events from occurring

*Translation:* expenditive wasteful spending of time/resource: prefer
'wastes time/resources X-ing' (e.g. 'wastes hours on idle speculation')

**LIM** limitative (§5.6.24)

Translates English 'to be/do X (just) in time' indicating an act/event
that culminates an anticipatory context

*Translation:* limitative just-in-time boundary: prefer 'just in time to
…' (e.g. 'left just in time to avoid the storm')

**EPD** expeditive (§5.6.25)

Conveys a sense of haste associated with an action or event

*Translation:* expeditive hurried manner: prefer 'in a hurry' /
'hastily' (e.g. 'they packed in a hurry')

**PTC** protractive (§5.6.26)

Conveys that an action, state or event takes place over a long period of
time

*Translation:* protractive long-ongoing: prefer 'for quite a while' /
extended duration so far (e.g. 'it's been snowing for quite a while')

**PPR** preparatory (§5.6.27)

Conveys that an action, state or event exists or takes place in advance
of another

*Translation:* preparatory in-advance: prefer 'planned/prepared X in
advance' (e.g. 'they planned the route in advance')

**DCL** disclusive (§5.6.28)

Focuses on the revelatory nature of an action, state or event

*Translation:* revelatory frame: prefer "it turns out …" / "as it turns
out"—do not omit the revelatory matrix

**CCL** conclusive (§5.6.29)

Conveys the direct outcome of an action, state or event within the
short-term context of the situation at hand

*Translation:* conclusive ended-up result: prefer 'we've ended up …' /
final landing (e.g. 'we've ended up in the old district')

**CUL** culminative (§5.6.30)

Similar to the conclusive above, but with a focus on the eventual,
long-term outcome over an extended period of time

*Translation:* culminative eventual outcome: prefer 'eventually wound up
…' (e.g. 'we eventually wound up in the old district')—contrast CCL's
more final 'ended up'

**TRD** tardative (§5.6.32)

Conveys the idea that an action, state, or event lessens, dwindles, or
slackens in energy, intensity, or effect

*Translation:* tardative gradual waning: prefer progressive decline over
time (e.g. 'enthusiasm wanes as the years go by')

**TNS** transitional (§5.6.33)

Focuses on the initial stage of preparation, adjustment, or
accustomization to an action, state or event

*Translation:* transitional taking-up new state: prefer 'has taken up X
as …' (e.g. 'has taken up painting as a pastime')

**ITC** intercommutative (§5.6.34)

Conveys the idea of "sequential reciprocity," meaning that the action or
event is a consequent reciprocation triggered by, or in reaction to, an
initiating action or event

*Translation:* intercommutative mutual return-act: prefer reciprocal 'X
back' (e.g. 'couldn't help but glance back with curiosity')

**MTV** motive (§5.6.35)

Conveys that an action, state or event involves physical removal or
absence of the participant from the present context of discourse

*Translation:* motive let's-head-off: prefer hortative motion 'let's
head off to X' (e.g. 'let's head off to the coast')

**SQN** sequential (§5.6.36)

conveys the idea a "sequential progressive" in which a series of
contextually identical instances is seen as comprising a single event

*Translation:* sequential one-by-one: prefer 'one by one' / itemized
sequence (e.g. 'checked each box one by one')

## Illocution

**DIR** directive (§6.1.1)

the statement is either an imperative command to another party to do/be
something, or a "commissive" statement committing the speaker to a
specific state or course of action

*Translation:* directive command: prefer imperative to the addressee
(e.g. 'Walk away from the platform!')

**DEC** declarative (§6.1.1)

a "performative" statement which. by its utterance/publication, creates
a change of state (at least psychologically) for the addressees

*Translation:* do not add a matrix 'I hereby declare that…' clause;
prefer direct announcement/presentation force (e.g. 'Here's…' / 'We
have…' / 'What a…!'); with compete.to.win+ACCESSOR-IND+SLF:BEN, announce
the winner in 2nd/3rd-person presentation force—never 1st-person 'I
declare myself the winner' / 'I've won'

**IRG** interrogative (§6.1.1)

a statement whose purpose is to inquire or seek information from the
addressee

*Translation:* interrogative content question: keep wh-question force on
the queried relation/time/place

**VER** verificative (§6.1.1)

a statement whose purpose is to seek/obtain corroboration, confirmation,
or verification

*Translation:* verificative yes/no check: prefer a polar question
seeking confirmation (e.g. 'Are the guests ready?')

**ADM** admonitive (§6.1.1)

a statement whose purpose is to provide advisory/admonitive information
to the addressee

*Translation:* admonitive warning: prefer 'Beware of …!' / warning
imperative (e.g. 'Beware of thin ice!')

**POT** potentiative (§6.1.1)

a statement of wishing, hoping, or other unreal(ized) provenance

*Translation:* when OBG (must/have to) + ACT co-occur, prefer obligation
("must") over stacking "hope" from POT

**HOR** hortative (§6.1.1)

a counterfactual statement indicating a desired but impossible state of
affairs that cannot be realized

*Translation:* counterfactual wish: "If only …"; with NEG on pathogen
stem + ADI illness, the wish is that the ABS party GET the disease—not
that they remain not-ill

**CNJ** conjectural (§6.1.1)

Equivalent to an English-language "if"-clause, indicating that the
statement is offered as a conjectured hypothetical

*Translation:* conjectural if-then speculation: prefer 'If you …,
there'll be …' conjectural consequence (e.g. 'If you enter the cave,
there'll be no easy return')

## Validation

**OBS** observational (§6.1.2)

present sensory knowledge or present sensory experience

*Translation:* epistemic source; do not add a separate "I perceive
that…" matrix clause when the clause already has an explicit case-marked
subject; on impersonal/eventive clauses with no case-marked actor,
prefer 'takes place' / 'there's … going on' — do not add 'is observed'

**REC** recollective (§6.1.2)

past sensory knowledge or past sensory experience — the speaker's
knowledge derives from prior direct perception or memory rather than
present observation

*Translation:* recollective remembered knowledge: keep as recalled fact
(e.g. 'A lived longer than B…' as remembered), not newly inferred

**PUP** purportive (§6.1.2)

knowledge from a definitive/(quasi-)verifiable 3rd party source

*Translation:* purportive alleged/yet-to: prefer 'had not yet …' /
purported timeline claim (e.g. 'cities had not yet formed in that era')

**RPR** reportive (§6.1.2)

knowledge from a 3rd party

*Translation:* reportive hearsay: prefer a hearsay matrix ('I'm told…' /
'I hear…') before the reported clause

**USP** unspecified (§6.1.2)

Validation deliberately unspecified — the speaker makes no commitment as
to the epistemic source or evidentiary basis for the statement

*Translation:* unspecified validation: no special evidential
matrix—render the clause plainly (e.g. a plain milestone statement)

**IMA** imaginary (§6.1.2)

unreal statement, not intended as true, based on whim, imagination,
dream, altered mental state, etc.

*Translation:* imaginary validation: treat as imagined/unreal (e.g.
'there's a dragon in the attic' as imaginary)

**CVN** conventional (§6.1.2)

cultural/conventional (i.e., collectively agreed-upon) knowledge

*Translation:* often surfaces as "They say…" / "It is said…"

**ITU** intuitive (§6.1.2)

intuition, hunch, subjective feeling, past experiences, etc.

*Translation:* When ITU validates a modal/nomic formative with ACT (no
separate speaker formative), render speaker intuition with a matrix like
'I think…' (do not omit it). When a case-marked subject already carries
perceptual content (OBS/see patterns), omit redundant matrix 'I
think/feel'.

**INF** inferential (§6.1.2)

inference from evidence (or absence of alternatives), induction,
extrapolation, etc.

*Translation:* inferential must-be: prefer 'must be …' inference (e.g.
'the guests must be ready')

## Case-Frame

**frame** case-frame (§6.2)

The New Ithkuil equivalent to relative or subordinate clauses is known
as a case-frame, or simply, frame.

*Translation:* case-frame: subordinate temporal/circumstantial frame for
the main clause (e.g. 'After the workers finish the shift, they must
…')—keep frame as adjunct, not the main predicate

**DSP** descriptive case (§6.2)

a noun in the descriptive case, which operates like RELATIVE case for

*Translation:* descriptive case-frame: relative/descriptive clause

## Slot V affixes

**V** Slot V affixes (§7.4)

Slot V affixes: If a CA complex is present in Slot VI of the formative,
any Slot V affixes are shownin reversed form: -CSVX-; however, if the
Slot IV VR and Slot VI CA complex have been elided as indicated by Slot
I (see Sec._3.10 on "short-cut" formatives), then any Slot V affixes are
shown in the standard form -VXCS-. As detailed earlier in Sec. 3.6.1,
the presence of a Slot V affix requires any Slot VI CA complex to be
geminated. ALSO: The semantic scope of any Slot V affixes applies only
to the formative Stem, not to its Slot VI CA affix-complex (i.e., the
various meanings of the CA affix-complex are applied to the formative
stem AFTER the stem has first been modified by its Slot V affixes).

*Translation:* Slot V affix string: affixes before Ca; e.g.
size+configuration stacked on a stem ('a group of large but otherwise
dissimilar boxes')

## Slot VII affixes

**VII** Slot VII affixes (§7.4)

Slot VII affixes are shown in standard form -VXCS- form, and apply to
both the stem AND its CA affix-complex (i.e., the meanings of the CA
affix-complex are applied to the formative stem first, then the
resulting stem+CA complex is then modified by any Slot VII affixes).
Note that in the absence of any Slot V affix(es), the presence of any
Slot VII affix(es) does not trigger the gemination of any Slot VI CA
complex.

*Translation:* Slot VII affix string: affixes after Ca; e.g. quantity
stacked outside configuration ('a large group of dissimilar boxes')

**pur** purposive (§7.6)

The entity/act/event which constitutes the inherent/innate/intrinsic

*Translation:* purposive function-as-name: English often names the tool
by purpose (e.g. 'a watering can')

nominal 'purpose for [authorized interference]'—not adverbial 'for the
purpose of…'

**INVERSE.ACCESSOR:PUR3** Type-3 inverse case-accessor (§7.6.3)

Type-3 inverse case-accessor affix for purposive case

*Translation:* Type-3 inverse purposive accessor: purpose-of reading
inverted (e.g. 'what using careful authority to intervene is for')

## Case-Stacking Affix

**CVS** conversive (§7.6.4)

Case-stacking affix used with conversive case; using the case-stacking
affix on unframed verbal formatives can function as a substitute for
case-frames

*Translation:* conversive exception: prefer 'except X' / 'all but X'
(e.g. 'everything was packed except the lamp')

**order** ordering of the formatives (§7.6.4)

ordering of formatives, placement of case-stacking affix, and use of TPF
affix

*Translation:* formative order/case-stacking: keep stacked modifiers
aligned to the intended head (e.g. looking at a book about capturing
animals)—do not reassign the about-clause to the wrong noun

## Adjunct

**SIN** single-affix (§8.1.1)

This adjunct associates a single VXCS affix to the following formative
and provides scoping information over the formative's other VXCS
affixes. The tell-tale sign of this adjunct is the V-C-(V) form
containing only one full consonant-form. Examples: ač, iakse, etra,
usmú, aull.

*Translation:* single-affix adjunct: one VxCs affix only; keep that
single modification (e.g. hurry alone → 'in a hurry')

**MUL** multiple-affix (§8.1.2)

associates two or more affixes to a formative

*Translation:* multiple-affix adjunct: stacked affixes; keep all stacked
senses (e.g. timing+avoidance together → 'just in time to avoid …')

**MOD** modular (§8.2)

has two slots corresponding to formative Slot VIII showing Valence,
Phase, Level, or Effect, in combination with Mood/Case-Scope, or showing
Aspect plus Mood/Case-Scope

*Translation:* modular adjunct: mood/module bundle; keep
modality+aspectual pieces (e.g. might + resume + random bursts)

**DSV** discursive (§8.3.2)

Indicates a phrase/statement represents direct speech

*Translation:* discursive adjunct: marks quoted/spoken discourse stretch
(e.g. turned and said "…")—keep as speech framing

**EXM** exemplificative (§8.3.5)

Indicates the word/phrase constitutes an illustrative example for the
preceding formative or phrase. Translates a clause in English beginning
with 'for example,...'

*Translation:* exemplificative adjunct: prefer 'for example, …' / 'e.g.
…'

**QUO** quotative adjunct (§8.4.2)

This adjunct combines the Carrier Adjunct with the discursive Register
Adjunct to allow direct quotes to be placed within a case-frame, useful
for translating imbedded quotes within sentences such as He told me "get
out of the house!"

*Translation:* quotative adjunct: marks verbatim quote content (e.g. a
commanded 'leave the room!')—keep quote force

## Bias Adjunct

**ANP** anticipative (§8.5)

Form: lst. 'I'm looking foward to this!'

*Translation:* anticipative bias: speaker anticipation/expectation color
(e.g. anticipatory 'I…' / awaiting tone)—do not invent extra
propositions

**COI** coincidental (§8.5)

Form: šš. 'What a coincidence!'

*Translation:* coincidental bias: prefer coincidence framing (e.g. 'What
a coincidence!')

**DEJ** dejective (§8.5)

Form: žž. '(sigh)' [of dejection/disillusionment]

*Translation:* dejective bias: weary/sigh affect (e.g. a parenthetical
sigh)—do not add propositional content

**DLC** delectative (§8.5)

Form: ẓm. 'Whee!'

*Translation:* delectative bias: playful delight (e.g. 'Whee!' / gleeful
tone)

**EUH** euphoric (§8.5)

Form: gzz. '(Sigh) What bliss!'

*Translation:* euphoric bias: blissful affect (e.g. '(Sigh) What
bliss!')

**EXG** exigent (§8.5)

Form: rrs. 'It's now or never!'

*Translation:* exigent bias: urgent/pressing tone on the clause (e.g.
clipped urgency)—do not invent a new main verb

**IDG** indignative (§8.5)

Form: pšç. 'The nerve!' or 'How dare…!?"

*Translation:* indignative bias: offense/affront (e.g. 'The nerve!')

**RPU** repulsive (§8.5)

Form: šš. 'Yuck! Ew! How gross!'

*Translation:* repulsive bias: disgust (e.g. 'Yuck! How gross!')

**SOL** solicitative (§8.5)

Form: ňň. 'please'

*Translation:* solicitative bias: please/request softener (e.g.
'please')

## Construction

**Type-2** Type-2 Concatenation (§10.1.1)

Type-2 concatenation is derivational (i.e., lexicalizing), creating a
new lexico-semantic gestalt usually requiring a different English
translation.

*Translation:* derivational concatenation: ROOT-'person'+child context
often yields 'child' (not bare 'person'); prefer the lexicalized English
reading
